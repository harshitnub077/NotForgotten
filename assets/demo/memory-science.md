# The Science of Memory and Forgetting

## The forgetting curve

The forgetting curve describes how quickly newly learned information fades when nothing is done to retain it. In the 1880s Hermann Ebbinghaus memorised lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself after different delays. He found that most forgetting happens soon after learning: within a day a large share of the material was gone, after which the decline slowed down. Modern analyses of millions of flashcard reviews show that forgetting is better described by a power law than by a simple exponential, because a population of memories with different decay rates averages into a curve with a long tail.

Retrievability is the probability that you can recall a memory at a given moment. Stability is the amount of time it takes for retrievability to fall from one hundred percent to ninety percent. A memory with high stability is forgotten slowly, while a fragile memory may be lost within days. Difficulty describes how hard a particular item is for a particular learner, and it controls how much stability can grow after each successful review.

## The spacing effect

The spacing effect is the finding that study sessions spread out over time produce far more durable memories than the same amount of study crammed into one session. Cramming can feel productive because recall is easy immediately afterwards, but that ease reflects short-term activation rather than long-term storage. Spaced repetition is a technique that schedules each review just before the memory is likely to be forgotten, so that every review happens at the moment it does the most good.

A review that happens when recall is still easy adds little stability. A review that happens when recall is difficult but still successful produces a large gain. This is why good schedulers let intervals grow: one day, then a few days, then weeks, then months. Each successful recall at a lower retrievability multiplies stability by a larger factor.

## Retrieval practice

Retrieval practice is the act of pulling information out of memory instead of putting it back in by re-reading. Testing yourself is not just a way of measuring learning; the act of retrieval itself strengthens the memory trace. In classic experiments, students who read a passage once and then practised recalling it remembered much more a week later than students who read it four times.

Generating an answer is more effective than recognising one. Typing a short answer from scratch forces deeper processing than choosing from multiple options or flipping a card and judging whether you "knew it". Feedback after retrieval matters too: seeing the correct answer immediately prevents errors from being consolidated.

## Interleaving and desirable difficulties

Interleaving means mixing different topics or problem types within a single study session instead of practising one type in a block. Blocked practice feels smoother, but interleaved practice teaches learners to discriminate between similar ideas and to choose the right strategy for each problem. Desirable difficulties are conditions that make learning feel harder in the moment but improve long-term retention, such as spacing, interleaving, and testing.

## Metacognition and the illusion of competence

Metacognition is the ability to monitor and judge your own learning. Learners are often poorly calibrated: re-reading creates a feeling of fluency that is mistaken for mastery, a problem known as the illusion of competence. Calibration measures how well your confidence matches your actual accuracy. A well-calibrated learner who says they are seventy percent sure is right about seventy percent of the time. Rating your confidence before checking an answer, and then comparing it with the outcome, is one of the fastest ways to improve calibration.

## Sleep and consolidation

Consolidation is the process that stabilises a fragile new memory into a durable long-term one. Much of this happens during sleep, when the hippocampus replays recent experiences and the neocortex gradually integrates them with existing knowledge. Studying before sleep and reviewing the next day exploits this process, which is another reason why spreading study across days beats a single long session.
